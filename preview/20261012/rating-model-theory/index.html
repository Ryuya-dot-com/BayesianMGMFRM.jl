How Rating Models Work

Unreleased documentation preview — 12 October 2026

This manual includes unreleased interfaces and examples. It is not the documentation of an installed release. Model support and scientific acceptance remain as stated on each page.

A rating model assigns a probability to each possible score. Its parameters describe how those probabilities change with the person, item and rater. This page builds the equations from a three-category example, then explains what generalization, multiple dimensions and Bayesian estimation add. The notation follows the implemented families; the detailed Model Equations page records their source conventions.

From adjacent categories to probabilities

Write $Y_{pir}$ for the rating of person $p$ on item $i$ by rater $r$. Let its categories be $0,\ldots,K-1$. For the partial-credit MFRM, define

\[x_{pir}=\theta_p-b_i-s_r, \qquad \log\frac{P(Y_{pir}=k)}{P(Y_{pir}=k-1)}=x_{pir}-d_{ik}, \quad k=1,\ldots,K-1.\]

SymbolMeaningEffect when other parameters are held fixed
$\theta_p$Person abilityLarger values favor higher scores.
$b_i$Item difficultyLarger values favor lower scores.
$s_r$Rater severityLarger values favor lower scores.
$d_{ik}$Step offset for the transition from category $k-1$ to $k$A larger offset makes that transition harder.

The ratio compares two adjacent exact categories. It is not the odds of scoring at least $k$. At $x_{pir}=d_{ik}$, categories $k-1$ and $k$ have equal probabilities, but either might be less probable than another category.

Recover all category probabilities by accumulating the adjacent log odds:

\[\ell_0=0, \qquad \ell_k=\sum_{m=1}^{k}(x_{pir}-d_{im}), \qquad P(Y_{pir}=k)=\frac{\exp(\ell_k)}{\sum_{h=0}^{K-1}\exp(\ell_h)}.\]

Setting the first log weight to zero chooses a reference; it does not make the probability of category zero vanish. Dividing by the sum makes the probabilities add to one. Rating-scale MFRM shares the step offsets across items; partial-credit MFRM allows item-specific steps. Ordered score labels do not require fitted adjacent-category step offsets to be increasing. Their interpretation differs from cumulative cutpoints in an ordered-logistic model.

Work through a three-category example

Consider categories 0, 1 and 2, with illustrative step offsets $d_{i1}=-0.5$ and $d_{i2}=0.5$. Set ability to 0.8 and item difficulty to 0.3. For rater severity 0.5, the net location is $x=0.8-0.3-0.5=0$. The adjacent log odds are 0.5 and -0.5, so the category log weights are $(0,0.5,0)$. Exponentiating and normalizing gives approximately $(0.2741,0.4519,0.2741)$.

A more severe rater lowers the score distribution while the person and item stay the same. These are conditional probabilities at chosen parameter values, not estimates from data or a claim that this particular prior or design is suitable.

This small Julia calculation reproduces the table without a package or MCMC:

using Printf

function category_probabilities(x, steps)
    logweights = vcat(0.0, cumsum(x .- steps))
    weights = exp.(logweights .- maximum(logweights))
    return weights ./ sum(weights)
end

steps = [-0.5, 0.5]
for severity in (1.5, 0.5, -0.5)
    x = 0.8 - 0.3 - severity
    probability = category_probabilities(x, steps)
    @assert isapprox(sum(probability), 1.0)
    @assert all(isapprox.(log.(probability[2:end] ./ probability[1:end-1]), x .- steps))
    @printf("severity=%4.1f  x=%4.1f  probabilities=(%.4f, %.4f, %.4f)\n",
        severity, x, probability...)
end
severity= 1.5  x=-1.0  probabilities=(0.5741, 0.3482, 0.0777)
severity= 0.5  x= 0.0  probabilities=(0.2741, 0.4519, 0.2741)
severity=-0.5  x= 1.0  probabilities=(0.0777, 0.3482, 0.5741)

Subtracting the maximum log weight before exponentiating preserves the probabilities and avoids unnecessarily large exponentials.

What GMFRM and MGMFRM change

The implemented scalar GMFRM uses the adjacent log odds

\[\log\frac{P(Y_{pir}=k)}{P(Y_{pir}=k-1)} =a_i c_r(\theta_p-b_i-s_r-d_{rk}), \qquad a_i>0,\ c_r>0.\]

Here $a_i$ is item discrimination and $c_r$ is rater consistency. Increasing their product multiplies all category log weights, concentrating probability on the most favored category or tied categories. It does not always move probability toward the highest score. Severity shifts where the distribution lies; consistency changes its concentration. Consistency is not a reliability coefficient, agreement percentage, or accuracy against an external standard. The steps $d_{rk}$ belong to the rater in this scalar model.

The implemented MGMFRM instead uses

\[\log\frac{P(Y_{pir}=k)}{P(Y_{pir}=k-1)} =1.7\,c_r\left(\sum_{d=1}^{D}a_{id}\theta_{pd}-b_i-s_r-d_{ik}\right).\]

There are $D$ named abilities for each person. A zero in Q fixes the corresponding $a_{id}$ to zero; an active entry permits a positive loading. The steps now belong to the item. The factor 1.7 is part of this implementation's source equation; scalar GMFRM above and fixed-coefficient MFRM use unit scaling. Item loadings in MGMFRM multiply abilities inside the sum, whereas scalar GMFRM discrimination multiplies the whole bracket. Thus raw parameter values cannot be compared across these families as though their units and priors were identical.

For fixed-coefficient multidimensional MFRM, the adjacent log odds are $\sum_d Q_{id}\theta_{pd}-b_i-s_r-d_{ik}$: active coefficients are one and there is no estimated consistency multiplier. This provides a distinct multidimensional alternative without estimating active loadings.

In MGMFRM, multiple active abilities enter an additive weighted sum. If an item uses reasoning and communication, a higher value in one can offset a lower value in the other. An interpretation requiring a person to exceed a separate minimum in both abilities would require a different response mechanism. The source discussion explains why terminology in an article must be read alongside its actual equation.

Compensatory and non-compensatory response rules

These words describe how abilities combine in the response equation. Imagine an item assessing reasoning and communication. In a compensatory model, stronger reasoning can offset weaker communication on that item, to an extent determined by its loadings. In a non-compensatory model, a weakness in a required ability limits performance even when another ability is strong. This need not be a literal pass/fail cutoff; the exact restriction depends on the response rule.

For the implemented MGMFRM, the relevant sum is $u_i=a_{i1}\theta_1+a_{i2}\theta_2$. With loadings $(2,1)$, profiles $(0,2)$ and $(1,0)$ both give $u_i=2$. Holding this item's remaining parameters and the rater fixed gives exactly the same category probabilities. This is a conditional tradeoff on one item. Other items with different loadings can distinguish these people; their full posteriors need not be the same.

For contrast, a binary product rule could be $P(Y=1)=\sigma(\theta_1)\sigma(\theta_2)$, where $\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$. Holding the second ability fixed bounds success probability by $\sigma(\theta_2)$, however large the first ability becomes. This illustrates a conjunctive restriction, but is not an ordinal model fitted by this package. No product, minimum or other operationally non-compensatory alternative is currently implemented.

Uto (2021) uses the term non-compensatory, while the published equation and Stan code aggregate abilities additively. We preserve the source terminology separately from this algebraic interpretation. Do not infer a conjunctive response mechanism from that label. The detailed source comparison links the article, original Stan code and contrasting response equations.

Within-item and between-item multidimensionality

These terms describe which abilities enter each item, rather than how those abilities combine. Here, within and between refer to items, not within-person versus between-person effects in a multilevel model.

Item structureExample Q rows for reasoning and communicationReading
Between-itemI1: (1,0); I2: (0,1)Each item measures one ability; different items address different abilities.
Within-itemI3: (1,1)This item measures both abilities.
Mixed in the package contractI1: (1,0); I2: (0,1); I3: (1,1)Pure and cross-loaded items coexist.

These rows illustrate the structure, not a sufficient calibration design. The ACER ConQuest manual uses within-item at test level when any item measures several dimensions. Our contract additionally distinguishes a mixed collection of pure and cross-loaded items. A small estimated positive loading is not the structural zero declared by Q.

A within-item model can use either a compensatory or a non-compensatory rule. In this package it uses the additive rule above. A between-item structure has no cross-ability tradeoff inside a single item, because that item uses only one ability; this does not make it a conjunctive model. Correlation between abilities is another separate choice: between-item does not mean uncorrelated, and within-item does not itself imply correlated population abilities.

The fixed-Q independent MGMFRM branch can represent cross-loaded items subject to its warning-bearing design and identification checks. Its current correlated 2D fitting route is restricted to between-item structure. In the gallery, each saved-fit item loads on only one dimension: its probability surface is flat along the other ability axis. A 3D display alone does not establish within-item measurement.

Why origins, units and priors matter

In the simple MFRM equation, adding the same constant to every ability and item difficulty leaves $\theta_p-b_i$ unchanged. The ratings alone cannot choose that common origin. Reference constraints and priors define the coordinates in which estimates are reported. Zero is a reference on that scale, not absence of ability or severity in an absolute sense.

Estimated loadings introduce further scale ambiguity: multiplying abilities by a positive constant and dividing their loadings by that constant preserves the weighted ability contribution. The MGMFRM ability prior fixes a population origin and marginal scale, while other constraints handle rater and step coordinates. That population convention does not force the observed persons' sample mean to zero or variance to one.

A proper prior can give a finite posterior even where the likelihood alone does not distinguish parameters. Finite intervals therefore do not prove that the data identify every separate effect. Compare meaningful contrasts on a declared scale, examine prior sensitivity, and consult Estimands, origins and units for the less obvious consequences of unequal loadings.

Prior SDs must also name their coordinate: an SD for a log loading is not an SD for a positive loading. A supplied ability prior SD is not an estimated population SD or a person's posterior standard error. The original generalized free-coordinate prior and the explicit normalized-prior alternatives can imply different distributions after constraints, including different treatment of raters. See Experimental Models before selecting their scales.

What Bayesian estimation computes

Let $\phi$ collect the unknown abilities, facet parameters and any estimated correlations. Bayesian inference combines a likelihood with a prior:

\[p(\phi\mid y)\propto p(y\mid\phi)\,p(\phi).\]

The likelihood describes observed scores conditional on the parameters. The prior describes their distribution before using these scores. The posterior describes their uncertainty after combining those ingredients. MCMC provides dependent numerical draws intended to approximate that posterior; increasing the draw count improves computation only when the sampler is exploring it adequately.

The baseline likelihood multiplies rating probabilities conditional on the modeled parameters. This assumes conditional independence, not independence of the observed scores before accounting for shared persons, items and raters. Unmodeled variation shared by ratings of the same response can violate that assumption. Merely storing a response identifier does not repair the likelihood.

Posterior prediction averages over parameter uncertainty:

\[P(Y^{\mathrm{new}}=k\mid y) =\int P(Y^{\mathrm{new}}=k\mid\phi)\,p(\phi\mid y)\,d\phi.\]

In practice, average the category probabilities over joint posterior draws. Plugging the posterior mean of each parameter into a nonlinear probability formula generally gives a different result. Prediction for an unseen person also needs that person's ability distribution; it is a different target from another rating of a person already represented in the fitted data.

How correlation and LKJ fit into the model

For several abilities, a population prior can use $\boldsymbol\theta_p\sim N(0,SRS)$. The diagonal matrix $S$ contains marginal SDs, while $R$ contains correlations. Correlation describes population association between abilities, not agreement between raters or causation.

An LKJ prior has density $p(R\mid\eta)\propto\det(R)^{\eta-1}$ on valid correlation matrices. At $\eta=1$ it is uniform over those matrices; for $\eta>1$ it favors matrices near identity. This does not mean all pairwise correlations are independently uniform. Its implications depend on dimension. Computations often use a lower-triangular Cholesky factor $L$ with $R=LL^\top$. Changing coordinates changes the density: the Cholesky density is generally not constant at $\eta=1$. See the Stan correlation-distribution reference.

These formulas extend mathematically beyond two dimensions. The package's fitting interface currently admits only its specified two-dimensional correlated models; a mathematical generalization does not establish a fitted or validated higher-dimensional workflow. Identity population correlation likewise does not make every posterior estimate or observed score independent.
